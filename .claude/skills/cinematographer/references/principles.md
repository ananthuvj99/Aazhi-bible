# Principles — how great cinematographers decide

These rules come from what working cinematographers say about their own work (Roger Deakins, Emmanuel Lubezki, Hong Kyung-pyo, Mátyás Erdély, Shyju Khalid, Madhu Ambat, Girish Gangadharan), from close study of directors' camera grammar (Kurosawa, Spielberg, Hitchcock, Kubrick, Scorsese, Cuarón, Fincher, Nolan, Steve McQueen), and from the video-essay and teaching tradition that analyses them (Every Frame a Painting, StudioBinder). Each rule ends with the check you run.

## 1. Motivation before movement

Deakins: if the camera moves, it has to be for a reason. He has also said that the reason can be emotion, not only something moving in the frame. Spielberg's version is literal: when a character reaches down for something, the camera tilts down with them.

The five motives:
- **Follow** — something moves and the camera goes with it.
- **Reveal** — new information enters: a place, a person, a threat.
- **Distance** — the emotional distance changes. Pushing in brings us closer; pulling out leaves the character alone.
- **State** — the camera expresses a mind: handheld panic, a floating dream, a spinning orbit.
- **Transition** — the move carries us to another time or place.

**Check:** name the motive in one word. If you can't, hold the frame.

## 2. Restraint makes the move land

A common way to study Deakins: shoot a dialogue scene with no movement at all, then add one slow push-in and see what it does. The discipline is restraint that pays off when you break it. His moves tend to be slow and heavy (creeping dollies, steady cranes) that let the frame breathe.

The Godfather shows why. The camera barely moves while Sonny and Tom argue after the attempt on the Don's life, so when Michael steps up, even a slight push on him feels grand.

**The static test.** Imagine the same shot locked on a tripod. If nothing is lost, hold it. If the feeling disappears (the unease of an arc around a menacing character, the awe of a tilt up to something huge), the move has earned its place.

**Check:** does this scene have one significant move, placed at its turn? Does every move pass the static test? If every shot moves, none of them means anything.

## 3. Stillness is a choice with power

A locked frame is not the absence of a decision. It can:
- let performance and dialogue command the screen (the coin-toss dialogue in No Country for Old Men);
- show off a composition that tells the story by itself;
- **trap** a character (Dunkirk's soldiers pinned between land and sea);
- refuse to comfort the audience. In 12 Years a Slave, the camera holds while Solomon hangs on tiptoe. Because it never moves to match his panic, the cruelty is harder to look at.

**Check:** if the shot is static, say what the stillness does: performance, composition, trap, or refusal.

## 4. Every move has a beginning, a middle and an end

Studying Kurosawa, Every Frame a Painting shows that each camera move has a clear start, middle and finish, and tells a story by itself. A move that starts nowhere and ends nowhere is drift.

**Check:** write the start frame and the end frame. If they say the same thing, the move isn't needed.

## 5. The frame can move when the camera doesn't

In Kurosawa's films there is almost always something moving in the frame: rain, wind, snow, smoke, crowds. That movement keeps a still camera alive. He composes five kinds of movement together: nature, groups, individuals, the camera, and the cut. Shyju Khalid uses rain, fog, sand and wind in the palms in Ee.Ma.Yau to carry what the characters feel, and he rejects stylish frames made only for effect.

For AI video this matters most of all. A held frame where curtains, water, smoke or crowds move often looks more cinematic, and generates more reliably, than a camera move across a dead frame.

**Check:** in every held shot, name what moves inside the frame.

## 6. Movement needs depth

Professional frames have layers: foreground, midground, background. Even a soft object near the lens adds depth. Nolan has said most of our depth cues come from occlusion, resolution and colour. When the camera moves, near layers slide past faster than far ones; that parallax is what makes movement feel three-dimensional. Truck past a doorframe and the shot comes alive. Truck along a flat wall and nothing happens.

**Check:** name the foreground, midground and background before planning any travelling move.

## 7. Lens and distance decide intimacy

- **Wide lens, close camera.** Lubezki says a wide lens very close to an actor makes the audience feel close, while long-lens close-ups feel artificial because you aren't really there. On The Revenant, very wide lenses kept the environment and the character's emotion in the same frame.
- **One lens, locked to the character.** Son of Saul used one 40mm lens with shallow focus, stayed at the protagonist's eye level, and stayed with him. Everything beyond him dissolves into blur.
- **Long lens, far camera.** Compression and separation: watching from afar, surveillance, the world pressing in on someone.

**Check:** choose observer (long lens, distance) or participant (wide lens, close) for each shot, on purpose.

## 8. Size equals importance

Hitchcock: the size of an object in the frame should equal its importance in the story at that moment. A push-in increases importance. A pull-out decreases it. An insert says "this matters."

**Check:** what is the most important thing right now? Is it the biggest, sharpest or brightest thing in the frame?

## 9. One move can mean several things — the context decides

- **Push-in** toward a face: realisation, decision, a point of no return (Michael in the restaurant in The Godfather). Toward an object: evidence (The Post).
- **Pull-out**: revealing context, an ending (Catch Me If You Can), or abandoning someone (Joker on the stairwell).
- **Arc**: uniting a group (The Avengers), panic in a small room (The Dark Knight), a mind spinning when it speeds up (City of God), one character's disorientation (Moonlight's opening).
- **Roll**: disorientation, or a power reversal (the Joker turned right side up while the world stays inverted).
- **Boom up / down**: a character's fortunes rising and falling in one take (Full Metal Jacket's obstacle course).
- **Zoom**: the unnatural eye, with no parallax. Slow zooms create dread (The Shining); crash zooms create comic or violent accents.

**Check:** state which meaning you intend. Make sure the scene's context supports it.

## 10. The camera has a point of view

Camera movement can work like narration:
- **first person** — POV, or locked to the character's head;
- **limited third person** — motivated by what the character does and feels;
- **omniscient** — the camera knows more than anyone in the scene; it reveals what nobody in the scene can see.

Distance is also point of view. Cuarón shot much of Roma in wide, held frames and slow lateral pans, often preferring medium shots where you'd expect close-ups. That lets the audience watch with empathy from a respectful distance, without nostalgia.

**Check:** whose camera is this — a character's, a companion's, or the storyteller's?

## 11. Camera style follows the character's arc

- **Goodfellas:** Steadicam glides and long takes while the life seduces Henry; handheld, jump cuts and paranoid zooms as addiction takes hold. Scorsese said the Copacabana take was Henry's seduction of Karen and the lifestyle seducing him.
- **Parasite:** a subtle forward creep while the family infiltrates; stillness once they're in; erratic handheld when the secret returns. Movement up and down stairs carries class.
- **Seven:** inside one scene, the panicking detective gets handheld while the calm killer gets locked-off frames. Two camera styles in one scene create friction.

**Check:** where is this scene on the film's camera arc? Does the camera behave as the arc says it should here?

## 12. Great films set their camera rules first

Son of Saul's makers agreed rules before shooting: the film must not look beautiful or appealing, one lens, always at the character's eye level, always with him. Shyju Khalid deliberately gave Nayattu lots of movement and many frames, and Joji steady tracking and travelling shots, so the two films would never be confused.

**Check:** does this project have a camera rulebook? If not, and it is more than a single shot, offer one (`camera-rulebook-template.md`).

## 13. A long take holds many shots

- Lubezki prefers to walk into the close-up from the wide shot rather than cut to it.
- A Spielberg oner moves from full shot to medium to close and back without cutting; the blocking and the camera are designed together.
- In Jallikattu, Girish Gangadharan's camera was hardly ever static, walking and running in sync with the characters. Angamaly Diaries ends on an 11-minute single take.
- Long takes are for real time, immersion and mounting tension. They work when the take passes through several compositions with a purpose, not when the camera just wanders.

**Check:** if it is a oner, list the compositions it passes through and why each one is there.

## 14. Cut on movement, end on stillness

Kurosawa cut on movement, so you follow the motion and don't notice the edit. He ended scenes on something static, then cut straight into movement, changing the rhythm.

**Check:** plan where a cut can hide inside a motion (a turn, a stand, a door), and where a sequence comes to rest.

## 15. Rhythm

Camera movement has tempo. Whiplash times its whip pans to the music. Wes Anderson's fast, precise pans and trucks feel like turning the pages of a storybook. A slow pan builds anticipation; a fast one adds energy.

**Check:** does the speed of each move match the scene's tempo, and the music if there is any?

## 16. Weight and believability

Real camera rigs have mass. A dolly eases in and settles; a heavy shoulder rig breathes rather than jitters; a crane decelerates at the top. Moves that start and stop instantly, or float without weight, read as computer-generated. Every move must also be physically possible for a real crew: a big dolly move doesn't fit inside a small room.

**Check:** which real rig would do this move, and could the space hold it?

## 17. The "make it cinematic" frame check

Run this on any frame before deciding how it moves. Fix the frame first; movement can't rescue a flat frame.

1. **Depth** — foreground, midground and background are all present, ideally separated by light and dark.
2. **Light with a source** — every light has a motivated origin (window, lamp, fire, sky). There is contrast, and the subject separates from the background.
3. **Life** — something moves besides the camera.
4. **Hierarchy** — size, focus and brightness point to what matters now.
5. **Composition** — thirds or deliberate symmetry, lead room, headroom, negative space used on purpose, frames within frames.
6. **Lens** — the FOV fits observer or participant.
7. **Restraint** — if the frame already says it, hold it.

## 18. The per-shot move check

1. **Motive:** follow / reveal / distance / state / transition — or hold. Run the static test: what would be lost if this shot were locked off?
2. **Axis:** rotate in place or travel through space — stated plainly.
3. **Arc:** start frame → path → end frame; ease in, settle.
4. **Depth:** layers named for parallax.
5. **Life:** in-frame motion named.
6. **Size = importance:** at the end of the move, the right thing is biggest or sharpest.
7. **Numbers:** speed, distance, height, duration, FOV.
8. **Point of view:** whose camera.
9. **Arc of the film:** consistent with the rulebook and where the story is.
10. **AI risk:** known failure and its fix, written into the prompt.
